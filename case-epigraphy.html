Reading the Naneghat inscription

What can a rock-cut inscription reveal about royal identity, ritual, and the changing forms of writing?

01 / The enquiry

Connecting a text to its place and time

This academic project examines the Naneghat inscription associated with Queen Naganika through site imagery, published copies, scholarly readings, and comparisons with Ashokan Brahmi. It asks how the inscription’s setting, content, and letterforms can be read together as historical evidence.

Completed for the Study of Epigraphy and Numismatics of India course.

02 / Site & context

A cave along a route of exchange

Naneghat lies in the Western Ghats, northwest of Junnar in Maharashtra. The pass connected western coastal ports with inland settlements, providing the geographical context for this study.

The evidence is organised into three groups: the inscriptions on the left wall, those on the right wall, and the shorter labels associated with portrait figures at the back of the cave.

03 / Research approach

Research method

  1. Site contextSite maps and photographs established the setting and the relationship between the inscribed surfaces.
  2. Documentary comparisonEarly hand copies were examined alongside photo-lithographs and later scholarly interpretations.
  3. Textual analysisThe analysis identified references to rulers, family relationships, sacrifices, and gifts.
  4. Script comparisonStroke length, angularity, letter heads, curves, and conjunct characters were compared with Ashokan Brahmi.
04 / The documentary record

From copied marks to published readings

The study traces early documentation to W. H. Sykes, who recorded the engravings in 1829 and published a partial hand copy in 1837. Such records provide another way to examine writing that is difficult to distinguish on a weathered surface.

James Burgess’s photo-lithographs supported Georg Bühler’s published transliteration and translation in Archaeological Survey of Western India, volume 5 (1883). Comparing these records brings both surviving characters and damaged areas into view.

05 / Interpreting the content

Royal identity, expressed through ritual

The reading centres on Naganika and her royal family. It identifies references to her husband Satakarni, her father, and her sons, alongside accounts of sacrifices and the gifts accompanying them.

More than a list of names

Cows, horses, chariots, garments, and coins appear among the recorded gifts. Read together with the royal names, these details connect dynastic identity with ritual activity and material resources.

The portrait labels

The shorter inscriptions repeat names found in the longer wall texts. Scholarly readings associate these labels with carved portrait figures.

Bhagavanlal Indraji documented six labels; the study also draws on V. V. Mirashi’s discussion of eight statue inscriptions.

06 / Comparative analysis

Looking closely at the letterforms

Comparison with Ashokan Brahmi focused on five features: stroke length, angularity, letter heads, curves, and variation in individual letters.

Vertical strokes

Some letters show shorter or more equal vertical strokes.

Angular forms

Certain samples display a more angular construction.

Letter heads

Flattened tops suggest possible early head marks, although the study notes no clear thickening of the edges.

Curves and loops

The study considers rounded and looped shapes as a tentative line of comparison.

Individual letters

The analysis highlights variation in letters including dha and ra, while noting no striking change in vowels or vowel marks.

07 / Synthesis

What the evidence brings together

The case study connects three scales of enquiry: a pass within a route of exchange, an inscription describing royal and ritual relationships, and individual letterforms that invite comparison with earlier writing.

The letterforms offer clues to the inscription’s historical context, but cannot establish a precise date on their own.

Where the reading stays open

The study leaves open questions about Naganika’s status as regent or widow and notes uncertainty around the inscription’s damaged opening line. Damage to the surface and differences between surviving records limit the certainty of these interpretations.
